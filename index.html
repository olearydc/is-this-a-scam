      What to do now

      • Do not click links, open attachments or call numbers given in the message.
      • Contact the company or person yourself, using a phone number or website you already know.
      • Never share a code, password or PIN, even with someone who says they are from your bank or the Gardaí (the police).
      • Ask someone you trust before you do anything.
      • If you have already clicked, given details or sent money, call your bank straight away, then report it at your local Garda station.
      How to report it, or what to do if you've been caught out

      If you haven't replied, clicked or paid: you don't need to do anything else. Don't reply. If it pretends to be a company, you can let that company know, using contact details from its own website or your bills.

      If you clicked a link, gave details or sent money:

      1. Call your bank straight away, using the number on your card or statement. Ask them to stop or reverse the payment and to put a hold on your account.
      2. Report it at your local Garda station. Find your local station.
      3. Keep the message. Don't delete it. Take a screenshot, and note the dates, amounts and any reference numbers. Your bank and the Gardaí may need them.

      Remember: Gardaí, banks and government departments will never ask for your PIN or password, or ask you to move your money.

      Useful numbers:

      • Someone at your door or in danger now: 999 or 112
      • Garda Confidential Line: 1800 666 111
      • CCPC consumer helpline, for advice: 01 402 5555
      • Age Action, for older people and their families: 01 475 6989
      • Your bank: the number on the back of your card

      Just want to check a website address? Try ScamChecker from FraudSMART, run by Irish banks.

      Someone is on the phone to you

      If they are asking for money, a code, your PIN or to install an app, it is very likely a scam.

      1. Hang up now. It's not rude. A real bank, Garda or company will understand.
      2. Wait 5 minutes, or use a different phone. Some scammers stay on the line and pretend to be the bank when you ring back.
      3. Ring back on a number you find yourself: on the back of your bank card, on a bill, or on the official website. Never the number they gave you.
      4. Talk to someone you trust before you do anything.

      A warning says you have a virus

      A page that says your computer or phone is infected and gives a number to ring is a scam. Your computer is almost certainly fine. Microsoft, Apple and your phone company never put a phone number in a warning like this.

      1. Don't ring the number and don't click anything on the warning.
      2. On a computer, hold down the Esc key (top left of the keyboard) for a few seconds. That gets you out of full screen. Then close the browser.
      3. If it won't close, switch the computer off with its power button, wait a minute, and switch it on again. Don't reopen the page it was on.
      4. On a phone or tablet, close that tab, or close the browser app.

      If you already rang them or let them in

      • Hang up. Disconnect the computer from the internet (switch off the Wi-Fi or unplug the cable).
      • If you paid or gave bank details, ring your bank straight away on the number on your card.
      • Ask someone you trust, or a local computer shop, to check the computer before you use it for banking.
      • Change your passwords using a different device, starting with email and banking.
      • Report it at your local Garda station. It's not your fault. These people are very good at what they do.
